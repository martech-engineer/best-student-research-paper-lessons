# Presentation notes

## Purpose

These are personal lessons from a Best Student Paper experience at SEMANTiCS 2026. I would present them as practices that helped strengthen the work, rather than a checklist that guarantees a prize. The through line is that research quality also depends on how well we revise, explain, share, and discuss the work.

## 1. The revision is where clarity happens

Reviewers reveal where the first version is difficult to follow. A set of weak accepts and an accept can mean the contribution is promising while parts of the argument still make readers work too hard. The important move is to rebuild the paper around that feedback: clarify the motivation, make the method legible, tighten the claims, and help researchers outside the immediate niche understand why the work matters. A good revision changes the reader's experience of the whole paper.

## 2. A concrete example can carry the method

Authors often understand an abstract pipeline because they have lived with it for months. New readers need one intuitive case that shows the input, what happens at each step, and the outcome. A well-designed figure can turn that example into a shared reference point. It should help readers understand the method and check their interpretation, rather than simply decorate the manuscript.

## 3. Coachability changes the paper

Invite experienced researchers to challenge the revised version before the proceedings deadline. The difficult part is to listen without treating criticism as a personal verdict. Try to understand what a fresh reader could not infer, then act on it quickly. Repeated exposure to this kind of review develops the ability to look at your own work with new eyes.

## 4. Own every sentence and every figure

AI can help with outlines, wording alternatives, and rough sketches. The final prose and visuals still require a researcher's care. Generic language or unfinished AI-style graphics can create a low-effort impression even when the underlying study took considerable work. Revise each assisted sentence for precision and natural voice; rebuild each figure so it explains this particular method or result.

## 5. Learn the conference conversation

Look at accepted papers, the program, and the questions the community returns to. That helps you gauge where your work sits in relation to the conference and how to introduce its relevance to people in adjacent areas. Use what you learn to shape the talk's opening and to ask more informed questions when you meet other researchers.

## 6. Interest starts before the session

The conference begins before you step onto the stage. Talk to people about their work, notice genuine overlaps, and invite relevant peers to your session. An engaged audience can produce better questions and more useful conversations afterward. In my experience, the interest around a talk can also influence how its success is perceived. The best invitations grow out of real curiosity.

## 7. Make the unfamiliar feel reachable

Start with a problem or idea the audience already recognizes, then reveal the novel application, method, or perspective. This familiar surprise gives people a foothold before asking them to follow a new contribution. Keep slides simple enough to understand while listening. The novelty should come from the research, not from an intricate layout the audience has to decipher.

## 8. Study talks as carefully as papers

Strong talks and speaking guidance are useful research material in their own right. I have learned from MIT's *How to Speak* and from examining how effective speakers organize a scientific story. One practical workflow is to extract principles from a transcript, make them into reusable instructions, ask an AI agent for a rough structure, and then rewrite and redesign it around your actual argument and voice.

## 9. An academic talk can be enjoyable

Conferences have long days and many sessions. A small joke, a surprising example, or a moment of warmth can renew attention without sacrificing rigor. Keep humor connected to the research and appropriate to the room. The point is to help people stay with the scientific argument and enjoy the process of understanding it.

## 10. Confidence needs a time buffer

Rehearse aloud and aim to finish the core talk before the allotted speaking time. If the schedule gives you fifteen minutes to present and five for questions, about twelve to thirteen rehearsed minutes can leave space for pauses, transitions, and small disruptions. Keep the talk substantial enough to do the work justice. The buffer makes it easier to speak with confidence instead of racing the clock.

## 11. The questions are part of the talk

Prepare for questions about what the solution already handles, its limits, and what comes next. Concrete answers and examples show command of the research beyond the rehearsed slides. When the answer is uncertain, explain what you would test next and why. This communicates both expertise and intellectual honesty.

## 12. Be proud and remain curious

Conference acceptance is an achievement worth celebrating. At the same time, stay interested in other people's work, respectful in conversation, and open to feedback. An award marks a moment in a longer research journey. The habits and relationships built around the work can matter well beyond that moment.

## Closing thought

Share what helped you improve. The value of an achievement grows when others can use what you learned on the way there.
